Water Bottling Line: Stages, Equipment and Cost Drivers

A practical guide to the mineral water bottling line — its stages, equipment, what really drives cost, and the decisions to settle before buying.

← Back to all articles

PRODUCTION LINES ·16 August 2026 ·8 min read

A water bottling line is a connected series of units that starts with raw water and ends with a sealed, labelled pack ready to ship. It runs through seven stages: treatment, sterile storage, bottle blowing, rinsing, filling, capping, then labelling and wrapping. On modern lines the rinse, fill and cap stages are combined into a single machine, which shortens the path the open bottle travels and reduces contamination risk.

This guide is written for anyone specifying a new line or upgrading an existing one. It concentrates on the engineering decisions that determine whether the project succeeds, rather than on machine datasheets.

The stages, in order

  1. Raw water intake from a borehole or mains supply into a receiving tank.
  2. Treatment and purification through filtration, reverse osmosis and sterilisation.
  3. Sterile storage in stainless tanks fitted with air filters.
  4. Bottle blowing from PET preforms, usually on site.
  5. Rinse, fill and cap in one combined unit.
  6. Labelling and coding with production date and batch number.
  7. Collation and wrapping into shrink packs or cartons.

Treatment is the stage that decides product quality

Final quality is set by the treatment plant, not by the speed of the filler. Plant design depends entirely on a laboratory analysis of the raw water: total dissolved solids, hardness, iron and manganese content, and microbial load.

A typical arrangement runs sand filtration to remove coarse solids, carbon filtration for chlorine and taste, ion exchange where hardness demands it, reverse osmosis to bring dissolved solids to target, and ozone or UV sterilisation immediately before filling.

The most common and most expensive mistake is buying a standard treatment package without an analysis, then discovering after installation that the water needs an additional stage. Retrofitting one into a commissioned plant is disruptive and costly.

Equipment on a water bottling line

  • Water treatment plant, sized from the water analysis.
  • Blow moulding machine converting preforms into bottles.
  • Rinse-fill-cap unit, the core of the line.
  • Labelling machine.
  • Coder for date and batch marking.
  • Wrapping unit, shrink or carton.
  • Conveyors linking every unit together.

Conveyor systems are routinely treated as a minor line item, when in practice they decide whether the installation behaves as one line or as a row of separate machines. Mismatched conveyor speeds between units are a frequent cause of stoppages on lines whose machines are all individually healthy.

What actually drives the cost

There is no standard price. Any figure quoted without the following is guesswork:

  • Throughput in bottles per hour — the single largest factor.
  • Degree of automation — semi-automatic against fully automatic.
  • Raw water quality — sets the size and stage count of the treatment plant.
  • Number of bottle formats — each additional format means change parts and changeover time.
  • Equipment origin and whether spare parts are available locally.

That last point is consistently underweighted. Cheaper equipment whose spares carry a long import lead time can cost more during its first breakdown, because a stopped line is measured in lost production rather than in the price of the part.

Choosing the right throughput

The instinct is to buy the largest capacity affordable. It is usually the wrong call. The correct figure comes from three numbers: realistic monthly market demand, the number of hours the plant will actually run each day, and expected availability.

A high-capacity line running two hours a day is not a fast line — it is idle capital. It is generally better to size for efficient running and leave physical space in the layout for an additional unit when demand grows.

Space and layout

Layout precedes machine selection. Four areas need separating clearly: the treatment area, ideally isolated from the production hall; the production hall itself with epoxy flooring and adequate drainage; raw material storage for preforms, caps and labels; and finished goods storage away from heat and direct sun.

Personnel and forklift routes, and maintenance access around every machine, must be in the plan. Counting only the machine footprints is a recurring cause of plants that cannot be serviced without shutting the whole line down.

Utilities: the cost that shows up monthly

Offers are normally compared on purchase price, while the real cost appears in the monthly running bill. Ask every supplier, in writing, for electrical load per unit under full running conditions, compressed air consumption and required pressure, the reject ratio at the reverse osmosis stage, and consumption of salt, filters and sanitising chemicals.

Two lines with identical throughput can differ considerably on these figures, and the difference repeats for the working life of the plant.

Quality control during production

A sound design has to be demonstrated daily. A workable minimum is a small in-house laboratory measuring pH, dissolved solids and turbidity; periodic samples drawn from both the start and the end of the line; and a retained sample from every batch held for a defined period.

All of it should be documented. Documentation is not merely a formality for inspectors — it is the only practical way to trace the source of a problem that surfaces weeks later.

Common mistakes when buying a line

  • Ordering machines before analysing the raw water.
  • Ignoring changeover time when multiple formats are planned.
  • Failing to match unit speeds, leaving a row of separate machines.
  • Holding no stock of fast-wearing spares.
  • Choosing a supplier with no local technical support.

The last carries the heaviest consequences. A line that cannot get an engineer the same day turns from a productive asset into a liability, which is why technical support and spare parts availability belong in the purchase decision rather than being treated as an afterthought.

Before you request a quotation

Prepare three things: a laboratory analysis of the raw water, a target throughput in bottles per hour, and the bottle formats required. With those, a quotation becomes realistic and genuinely comparable between suppliers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the stages of a water bottling line? +
Seven, in sequence: raw water intake, treatment and purification, sterile storage, bottle blowing from PET preforms, rinse-fill-cap, labelling and coding, then collation and wrapping. On modern lines the rinse, fill and cap stages sit in a single Blow-Fill-Cap machine, which reduces contamination risk and increases speed.
What determines the cost of a water bottling line? +
Four things: throughput in bottles per hour, degree of automation, the quality of the raw water — which sets the size of the treatment plant — and the number of bottle formats. A line cannot be priced sensibly without a laboratory analysis of the raw water and a target throughput, because the treatment plant alone can account for a large share of the total.
Should bottles be blown in-house or bought ready-made? +
In-house blowing is standard from mid-capacity upwards. Preforms take a fraction of the storage space of finished bottles, a freshly blown bottle enters the line immediately with less exposure to contamination, and changing format becomes a mould change rather than a supplier negotiation. The trade-off is added electrical load and a high-pressure air compressor, both of which belong in the utilities calculation from the start.
Can an existing bottling line be upgraded instead of replaced? +
Usually yes. Much installed equipment is mechanically sound but electrically dated, so modern PLCs, sensors and drives can be fitted to the existing frames. That costs less and causes far less downtime than a full replacement.
What utilities does a bottling line need? +
Ask every supplier in writing for the electrical load per unit under full running conditions rather than nameplate rating, compressed air consumption in cubic metres per minute with the required pressure, the water reject ratio at the reverse osmosis stage, and consumption of salt, filters and sanitising chemicals. Two lines of identical throughput can differ substantially on these, and the difference recurs every month.
