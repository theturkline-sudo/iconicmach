Conveyor Belt Types: How to Choose by Application

A practical guide to conveyor belt types used in factories — flexible, modular and roller families, and how to match the belt to your product and line.

← Back to all articles

CONVEYORS ·2 September 2026 ·7 min read

Conveyor belt types used in factories fall into three families: flexible belts made of rubber, PVC or polyurethane; modular plastic belts built from interlocking links; and roller conveyors that move loads over rotating rollers. The choice between them is driven by the nature of the product, its weight, operating temperature, hygiene and food-contact requirements, incline angle and the washdown environment. The common mistake is selecting a belt on price alone, then paying the difference later in damaged product and repeated stoppages.

The belt itself is one component inside a conveyor system that also includes the frame, pulleys, drive, take-up unit and controls. Choosing the right belt type does not remove the need to match the rest of the system to the same requirements, but it is the hardest decision to reverse after installation, which is why it should be settled first.

Conveyor belt types: the three families

Before getting into materials and surfaces, it helps to fix the overall frame. Most of what is installed in factories belongs to one of three families:

  • Flexible belts: a continuous band of rubber or polymer running around two pulleys. The most common choice for moving packs and bulk product along a straight path.
  • Modular plastic belts: interlocking plastic links that assemble and dismantle piece by piece, tolerate washdown and curves, and are driven by toothed sprockets.
  • Roller conveyors: rows of rotating rollers that move flat-based loads such as cases and pallets, either by gravity or by a drive.

Within each family, choices of material, surface and internal construction determine how the belt behaves with your product specifically, and those are covered in the sections below.

Flexible belts: rubber, PVC and polyurethane

This is the widest family, and its variants differ mainly in material and internal construction.

Rubber belts

The traditional choice for heavy loads and abrasive or sharp material, and for outdoor environments exposed to sun and dust. The belt is usually built from internal fabric plies that give it tensile strength, wrapped in a rubber compound that resists wear. Compounds vary in their tolerance of heat, oil and ozone, so they are selected to suit the operating environment: an oil-resistant compound is wasted in a stone quarry, while a general compound will not survive constant grease contact. The surface is available smooth or with a chevron pattern to lift bulk material on an incline, and moulded rubber edges can be added to stop granules scattering. Rubber is not normally used for direct food contact unless the compound is approved for that purpose.

PVC belts

The most common belt in light manufacturing, assembly, packing and dry-goods handling. Economical, and available with several surfaces: smooth, rough non-slip, and cleated. Its temperature and chemical range is narrower than polyurethane, and its cut edges can expose the internal fabric, so sealed edges or edge treatment are needed in wet environments.

Polyurethane (PU) belts

The usual choice for washdown food and pharmaceutical lines. Polyurethane resists fats and oils, tolerates cutting, and is available in fully homogeneous versions with no exposed internal fabric at the edges, which reduces bacteria-trapping sites and makes inspection easier. It costs more than PVC of the same size, offset by a longer life in a frequent-washdown environment.

Felt, fabric and mesh belts

Felt is used in glass and bakery cooling for a surface soft enough not to mark the product and for moderate heat resistance. Wire mesh and metal belts are used inside ovens, cooling tunnels, drying and draining operations, where air or liquid must pass through the belt itself. These belts vary in weave density, wire gauge and metal type, and are chosen by temperature, load and the size of the smallest piece that must not fall through the openings. Their drawback is that they are heavier and less flexible, need larger-diameter pulleys, and are harder to repair locally than splicing a polymer band.

Modular plastic belts and cleated belts

A modular belt is made of interlocking plastic modules, like jigsaw pieces, driven by a toothed sprocket (positive drive), so it does not wander off its path and needs no constant tension adjustment. When part of it is damaged, the worn section alone is replaced rather than the whole belt. Its construction lets air and water pass through, and it tolerates heavy washdown and horizontal curves. This is why it dominates beverage filling, poultry and dairy lines, and canning.

Cleated belts carry transverse flights, welded or moulded onto the belt surface, that lift product up an incline without it sliding back. Corrugated side walls are sometimes added to lift bulk product at steep angles. In return, the gaps between cleats are harder to clean, so they are avoided where hygiene is critical.

Roller and metal conveyors

A roller conveyor moves loads over a row of rollers. The gravity type relies on a slight slope and a manual push; the driven type turns the rollers with a chain or belt. It suits cases, totes and pallets with a flat, rigid base, and is not suitable for loose or soft product that would drop between the rollers. Its advantages are low energy use in the gravity type, the ease of splitting the path into accumulation and sorting zones at line ends and shipping areas, and fewer moving parts exposed to damage than a continuous belt.

Metal belts and hinged steel chains are used where polymers cannot cope: high heat in heat treatment and large bakeries, moving swarf and chips under cutting machines, and environments that would cut or puncture any flexible surface. Their cost and limited flexibility are offset by durability the other materials cannot provide.

Ways to join the belt ends

The splice method matters as much as the belt type, and the joint is often the first thing to fail. The two main options on flexible belts are:

  • Endless splice (thermal welding or vulcanising): the two ends are fused into a homogeneous joint close to the belt's own strength. It runs the smoothest and suits high speeds, abrasion and food contact, but it needs the belt removed or an on-site welding tool and a longer stoppage.
  • Mechanical fasteners (hooks or metal clips): faster to fit and replace without special equipment, and they let the belt be opened to service the frame. In return they create a small bump passing over the pulley, can catch fine material, and are less suited to hygienic washdown.

Modular belts need no splice in this sense; the loop is closed with a hinge rod that is pulled out to open it for maintenance. When requesting a quotation, state the required joining method explicitly, because it affects installation time and the later maintenance schedule.

How to choose the right type for your line

There is no belt that is best in the abstract; the best is the one closest to your application's conditions. Work through these factors in order:

  1. Product nature: rigid pack, bulk solid or soft piece? Sharp or abrasive? Greasy or wet?
  2. Load and speed: weight per metre, target throughput, and conveying length.
  3. Temperature: does the belt pass through an oven or cooling tunnel, or receive hot product?
  4. Hygiene and washdown: daily washing with water and chemicals favours polyurethane or modular belting over PVC with exposed fabric.
  5. Food contact: if the belt touches food directly, the material must be approved for that purpose (see the next section).
  6. Incline angle: above a certain angle product slides, so cleats, a rough surface or side walls are needed.
  7. Curves and path: a horizontally curved path suits modular or a dedicated curved belt more than a flat band.
  8. Environment: dust, oil, sunlight and ozone all shorten the life of particular compounds.

When in doubt, the practical order is to fix the material first by hygiene and temperature, then the construction by path and incline, then the surface by product. Width, thickness and tensile rating are derived from load and length after the type is fixed, not before. Avoid fixing the type on the basis of what a neighbouring factory uses: a similar product does not mean a similar speed, conveying length or washdown schedule, and those are the details that govern the choice. Food-contact and hygiene considerations

In Egypt, food facilities are overseen by the National Food Safety Authority, and food-contact-material rules can be updated. Verify the current requirements with the authority directly before specifying any belt material for a food line, and do not base the decision on an old specification or a supplier's word.

At a general engineering level: belts intended for food contact are made of non-toxic materials with a solid surface that resists bacterial penetration, and are preferably homogeneous with no internal fabric exposed at the edges. Modular belts and homogeneous polyurethane are easier to clean and inspect than PVC with fabric plies. Frequent washdown also calls for an open frame structure that allows drainage and drying, so water does not pool under the belt.

What drives the cost of a conveyor belt

There is no single price; cost is built from elements you can estimate in advance:

  • Material and construction: homogeneous polyurethane and modular belting cost more than PVC of the same size, offset by a longer life under washdown.
  • Width and length: these set the quantity of material and the size of the pulley and drive.
  • Surface and add-ons: cleats, side walls, perforation and coatings are all additional manufacturing operations.
  • Pulley, drive and take-up: a heavier belt needs more pull capacity and a stronger structure.
  • Supply and spares: local availability of spare parts lowers the cost of downtime later.

The useful comparison is not between two belt prices, but between the full cost over the belt's life: material plus energy plus downtime plus scrap plus replacement.

Signs of belt wear and when to replace

A belt signals the end of its life before it tears. Watch for: frayed edges rubbing against the frame, surface cracking or glazing, stretch beyond the take-up range, delamination on flexible belts, persistent mistracking despite adjustment, rising motor current for the same load, and increasing marking or scratching of the product.

On a modular belt the worn section alone is replaced and the line is back quickly. The same signs recurring along a flexible belt mean it is spent and patching will not help. See technical support for a simple periodic inspection schedule. And if the structure itself no longer suits the current product mix, a new conveyor system within an updated production line can cost less than successive upgrades to a frame that was never designed for them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What conveyor belt types are used in factories? +
They fall into three families: flexible belts (rubber, PVC, polyurethane, felt, wire mesh), modular plastic belts, and roller conveyors. Within each family, choices of material, surface and internal construction determine how the belt behaves with a specific product.
What is the difference between a PVC belt and a polyurethane belt? +
PVC is economical and common in assembly, packing and dry-goods handling, but its temperature and chemical range is limited and its cut fabric edges can fray. Polyurethane resists fats, oils and cutting, and comes in homogeneous versions with no exposed internal fabric, which suits washdown food and pharma lines at a higher cost.
Which belt type suits food production lines? +
In practice the choice is between homogeneous polyurethane and modular plastic belting, because both have a solid surface that is easy to clean and inspect and tolerates frequent washdown. Any material in direct food contact must be approved for that purpose; verify current requirements with the National Food Safety Authority directly before specifying.
When should I choose a modular belt instead of a rubber belt? +
When the path includes horizontal curves, when heavy washdown and liquid drainage are needed, or when stopping the line to replace a full belt is costly. Modular belts are repaired by swapping damaged modules, and their sprocket drive prevents mistracking. Rubber belts remain better for abrasive, heavy or sharp material and outdoor environments.
What factors decide which conveyor belt type to use? +
Product nature (rigid pack, bulk solid or soft piece; sharp or abrasive; greasy or wet), load and speed and conveying length, operating temperature, hygiene and food-contact requirements, incline angle, curves in the path, and environmental conditions such as dust, oil and sunlight.
When should a conveyor belt be replaced rather than repaired? +
When several signs appear together: frayed edges, surface cracking or glazing, stretch beyond the take-up range, delamination, persistent mistracking despite adjustment, rising motor current, and increasing marking of the product. On modular belts the worn section alone is replaced; the same signs recurring along a flexible belt mean the whole belt is spent.
