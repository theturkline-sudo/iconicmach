Packaging Machine Types and How to Choose One

A guide to packaging machine types — by product, dosing method, pack format and automation level — and how to choose the right one for your line.

← Back to all articles

PRODUCTION LINES ·3 September 2026 ·8 min read

A packaging machine is not a single category but a wide family, classified along three overlapping axes: how the product is dosed (weighing, auger for powders, volumetric and piston for liquids, counting for pieces); the pack format and forming technology (vertical VFFS, horizontal HFFS and flow wrap, rotary for pre-made pouches, linear for rigid containers); and the automation level (manual, semi-automatic, fully automatic). Any machine you choose is one decision on each axis, and the starting point is always the product itself, not the machine.

The common mistake is choosing a machine from a supplier catalogue and then trying to fit the product and pack to it. The correct order is the reverse: fix the product state, the pack size and the target output, and from those derive the dosing method, then the machine format, then the automation level. That order makes supplier quotes comparable on one basis, and stops you paying for capabilities your product does not need.

Packaging machine types: how they are classified

No single classification is enough, because every machine is described by three independent attributes. A coffee-bag machine, for example, might be: weigh dosing + vertical forming + fully automatic. An oil machine filling pre-made containers might be: piston dosing + linear filling + semi-automatic. Understanding the three attributes makes it possible to compare quotes rather than relying on a brand name.

Classification by dosing method

The dosing method is governed by the product and is the first decision:

  • Weigh dosing: multihead or linear weighers that weigh each batch before dropping it. The most accurate for irregular granules such as nuts, snacks and cereals.
  • Auger dosing: a rotating screw pushes a metered amount of powder. For flour, spices, milk powder and powder detergents.
  • Volumetric and piston dosing: a cylinder or piston delivers a fixed volume. For liquids, pastes, sauces and oils.
  • Count dosing: counts single pieces (biscuits, tablets, small parts) rather than weighing them.
  • Measuring-cup dosing: simpler and cheaper but less accurate, used for uniform granules when precision is not critical.

Choosing between these methods is a trade-off between accuracy, speed and cost. Multihead weighers give the highest accuracy and the least giveaway on valuable product, but are the most expensive and take a lot of vertical space. A measuring cup is cheap and fast but drifts more, so it overfills to avoid falling under the legal weight — and that overfill is a hidden cost that adds up daily. Auger dosing is accurate with powders but needs dust control and can pack down with damp materials. Settling the dosing method early determines the rest of the machine's specification, because the doser, screw or piston is built into its core rather than added later.

Classification by pack format and forming technology

  • Vertical (VFFS): forms the bag from a film roll, fills from the top and seals vertically. Economical and fast for free-flowing products, and the most common in dry-food packing.
  • Horizontal (HFFS) and flow wrap: forms the bag or wraps the product on its side. For single solid or fragile pieces: biscuits, energy bars, shaped frozen products.
  • Rotary pre-made pouch: opens ready-made bags (stand-up, zipper), fills and closes them on a rotary table. For packs with a premium retail appearance.
  • Linear and rotary for rigid containers: fills bottles, jars or tubs then closes them with a cap or foil seal. For liquids, dairy products and pickles.
  • Stick and sachet machines: small single-dose bags for sugar, coffee, ketchup and pharmaceuticals.

The machine format decides things beyond appearance: a vertical machine uses more film per pack because of the back seal, but its initial cost is lower and format changes are quicker. A horizontal machine is gentler on fragile product and gives a neat flat pack, in exchange for a longer footprint and a higher price. Pre-made pouches give a premium retail look and a reclosable seal, but a ready pouch costs more than a film roll. Rigid containers require a closing station (cap or foil) and bottle handling, which is a different route from bags entirely. This is why the pack format is fixed before any quotes are compared.

Classification by automation level

  • Manual: the operator performs each step with a dosing or sealing aid. For very small volumes and project start-ups.
  • Semi-automatic: the machine performs one or two steps while the operator loads or discharges. Flexible with changing formats and lower cost.
  • Fully automatic: forming, dosing, sealing and discharge with no intervention, at high output and consistency, and usually linked to conveyors and downstream equipment on the line.

Automation level is not a measure of "quality" but of fit to volume: a line producing small, changing formats may be served better by semi-automatic than by a full machine that spends its time changing over. A fully automatic machine also needs a higher-skilled operator to set up and maintain, and more specialised spares; if that support is not available locally, high output on paper turns into a long stoppage at the first fault. Upgrading from semi to fully automatic later is possible on some designs, so ask the supplier explicitly about the upgrade path before buying.

The three decisions — dosing, format, automation — are not equally hard to change: automation level can sometimes be raised with add-ons, and pack format can be widened with change parts, but the dosing method is built into the heart of the machine and is hard to swap. So the dosing method is settled first, and with the most allowance for the future.

How to choose the right machine

Work through these points in order before requesting any quote:

  1. Product state: granular, powder, liquid, viscous or a solid piece? This sets the dosing method.
  2. Pack format: formed bag, pre-made pouch, bottle, jar or sachet? This sets the machine format.
  3. Target output: packs per hour from confirmed demand, not ambition, with a margin for stoppages.
  4. Number of formats: one product at a fixed size, or several? Variety favours flexibility and quick-change parts.
  5. Film or pack material: its type, thickness and sealability; the machine is set up for a specific spec.
  6. Automation level: derived from output, format stability and labour availability — not chosen first.
  7. Line integration: how does product reach the machine and leave it? See conveyor systems to match infeed and discharge speed.

Note that this order starts from the product and ends at the machine, not the other way round. Most stalled purchases began with an attractively priced machine quote, and only after delivery was it found not to suit the product viscosity, the pack size or the line speed. Fixing the specification in writing before comparing quotes makes the comparison fair and guards against this mistake. Our engineers can match these factors to your product on a site visit before purchase — request a quotation.

A single machine or an integrated packing line

A packing machine can be bought on its own to add to an existing setup, or as part of an integrated line that runs from product feed to a shipping-ready case. The difference is not only price:

  • A single machine is faster to supply and cheaper, but assumes the feed, discharge, date coding and bundling are already solved. If they are not, the machine stands idle waiting on what comes before and after it.
  • An integrated line guarantees speeds match between stations and puts one supplier responsible for overall performance, in exchange for higher cost, a longer lead time and more space.

The practical rule: if you have a stable existing line and are adding one product, a single machine is usually enough. A new project or a large expansion is better served by an integrated line, because it removes the risk of mismatch between equipment from different suppliers.

What the machine needs around it

The machine arriving at the factory is not the end of the setup. Much start-up delay is caused by utilities or supplies that were not prepared in advance. Make sure you have:

  • Compressed air, dry and clean, at adequate pressure and flow for dosing, sealing and cutting.
  • Stable electrical supply at the required voltage, with a sound earth for the controls.
  • Film or packs to a consistent spec; roll variation between suppliers causes poor seals and stoppages.
  • A trained operator for format setup, part changes and daily maintenance.
  • Space around the machine for loading, maintenance and storing film rolls nearby.
  • A date-coding printer, integrated or standalone, for production and expiry dates and batch number.

Food-contact parts must be of approved materials; verify food-contact requirements with the National Food Safety Authority directly rather than relying on a supplier's word.

Complementary equipment on the packing line

A packing machine rarely works alone. It is usually completed by:

  • A checkweigher that rejects packs outside the allowed weight.
  • A metal detector after filling, for consumer safety and compliance.
  • A labelling machine for rigid containers.
  • A shrink tunnel to group packs into bundles.
  • A case packer to pack bundles into cartons.
  • Transfer and accumulation conveyors linking these stations; selection detail is on the technical support page.

What drives the price of a packaging machine

There is no single price; cost is built from several elements:

  • Automation level and output: a faster, more complete machine needs more actuators, control and a stronger structure.
  • Dosing method: multihead weighers cost far more than a measuring cup.
  • Number of formats and change parts: each extra pack shape means tooling and setup.
  • Construction materials: full stainless steel versus a painted frame, important for wet and food environments.
  • Add-ons: date coding, gas flushing or vacuum, an integrated metal detector.
  • Supply and spares: local availability of spare parts and support lowers lifetime downtime cost.

The useful comparison is not between two machine prices, but the full cost: the machine plus film and waste plus energy plus labour plus downtime and maintenance over its life. A cheaper machine with an unreliable seal or poor dosing accuracy eats the difference in film waste and overfill within months — and none of that shows in the price quote.

Common mistakes when buying a packaging machine

  • Choosing the machine before fixing the product and pack spec, so it arrives and does not fit.
  • Over-specifying speed so the rest of the line cannot feed it, and it runs at partial load.
  • Ignoring the film spec, so performance changes with every roll from a different supplier.
  • Not accounting for changeover time on a multi-product line, so production hours are lost to setup.
  • Buying without a spares and local-support plan, so a minor fault becomes an extended stoppage.

These are items that can be avoided at the technical-proposal stage. If you are setting up a new line or an expansion, our team can review the proposed production line and match the packaging machine to the other stages before you contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main types of packaging machine? +
They are classified along three axes: how the product is dosed (weighing, auger for powders, volumetric or piston for liquids, counting for pieces); the pack format and forming technology (vertical VFFS, horizontal HFFS or flow wrap, rotary pre-made pouch, linear for rigid containers); and the automation level (manual, semi-automatic, fully automatic). Any given machine is a combination of one choice on each axis.
What is the difference between a vertical (VFFS) and horizontal (HFFS) packaging machine? +
A vertical machine forms a bag from a film roll, fills it from the top and seals it, suiting free-flowing products such as grains, powders and liquids. A horizontal machine forms the bag or wraps the product on its side, suiting single solid or fragile pieces that need a stable base and gentle handling, such as biscuits and energy bars.
What is the difference between a semi-automatic and a fully automatic machine? +
A semi-automatic machine performs one or two steps while the operator loads bags or places product — cheaper, slower, and better for changing formats and mid-range volumes. A fully automatic machine forms, doses, seals and discharges with no intervention, at higher output and consistency, in exchange for higher cost and maintenance complexity.
Which packaging machine suits powders and liquids? +
Fine or airborne powders need an auger doser with a vertical machine plus compressed air and dust extraction. Liquids and viscous products need a volumetric piston or pump doser, drip-free filling nozzles, and often a linear or rotary machine for pre-made rigid containers.
How do I choose the speed of a packaging machine? +
From the target daily output divided by real operating hours, with a margin for stoppages, format changes and maintenance. A machine faster than needed costs more and demands an upstream feed the rest of the line may not provide; one that is slower becomes the bottleneck. Match its speed to the stages before and after it, not to an ideal figure.
Can one machine pack more than one product? +
Yes, if the products are close in physical state and pack size, with the differences handled through stored formats (recipes) and quick-change parts. But combining a powder and a liquid, or a small sachet and a large container, on one machine lengthens changeover and lowers efficiency — two dedicated lines may cost less.
