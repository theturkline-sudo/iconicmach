Dairy Production Line: Stages, Equipment and Cost Drivers

A practical guide to the dairy production line — stages from milk intake to packing, core equipment, CIP, utilities, and what drives cost.

← Back to all articles

PRODUCTION LINES ·2 September 2026 ·8 min read

A dairy production line is a continuous processing chain that begins with chilled raw-milk intake and ends with a packed product held cold. Its core stages are intake and quality testing, filtration and clarification, cold storage, fat standardisation by separation, homogenisation, pasteurisation or heat treatment, product manufacture (liquid milk, yoghurt, cheese, butter or cream), then filling, packing, cooling and storage. A cleaning-in-place (CIP) system and a set of utilities — steam, chilled water, compressed air — tie the stages together. The line's size and product range are what determine how many machines it needs and how complex they are, not the other way round.

The differences between one line and another come from three decisions: which products will be made from day one, the target daily capacity, and whether heat treatment is short-hold pasteurisation or UHT for a long ambient shelf life. Everything else is specified on top of those three.

Dairy production line: an overview of the route

Any dairy line can be divided into three zones: raw-milk reception and pre-treatment, heat treatment and homogenisation, and product manufacture and filling. Strict hygiene boundaries separate the zones — clean and unclean routes must not meet. Every machine on the line is wired to the control system to log temperature, time and pressure, because those readings are the food-safety evidence later.

Milk is sensitive to heat, to time and to contamination, so the line is designed to minimise how long untreated product sits and to prevent any stagnation in the pipework. The distance between intake and pasteurisation is kept as short as possible, and pipe diameters and pump speeds are calculated so that fat does not settle and protein is not broken by excess mechanical stress. These details do not appear on an equipment list, but they separate a line that runs steadily from one that suffers recurring contamination and unexplained losses.

Plant layout and zone separation

The arrangement of the building comes before the arrangement of the equipment. The governing principle is one-directional flow: raw milk enters from one side and packed product leaves from the opposite side, without the raw route ever crossing or passing over a clean area. Separate rooms are assigned to intake, processing, fermentation, filling, cold storage, and staff and utility areas, with barriers and air pressures that stop contamination moving between them.

Layout affects cost and operation directly: floors with correct slopes and drainage, washable walls, enough height to run pipework and conveyors, and movement routes that do not force a raw-milk trolley through the filling room. Correcting a poor layout after construction is close to impossible, so it is worth reviewing the plant drawing with the supplier before the concrete is poured.

Stages of a dairy production line, in order

1. Intake and quality testing

Raw milk arrives in refrigerated tankers and is tested before it is unloaded: temperature, acidity, density, fat and solids, adulteration with water or preservatives, and antibiotic residues. Milk that fails is rejected at the gate, not after it has been pumped into the tanks, because mixing it with sound milk spoils the whole batch and stops the line for an unscheduled clean. Every delivery's results are logged against the supplier, and those records form part of the traceability system.

2. Filtration, clarification and cold storage

Milk passes through a strainer and then a centrifugal clarifier that removes fine sediment and somatic cells. It is then held cold in insulated tanks before processing, which gives flexibility in scheduling production and holds back bacterial growth.

3. Separation and fat standardisation

The separator, a high-speed centrifuge, splits milk into cream and skim, which are then recombined to the fat content each product requires. Surplus cream goes to other products such as butter or packed cream.

4. Homogenisation

The homogeniser forces milk under high pressure through a narrow gap, breaking fat globules down to a fine, uniform size. This prevents a cream layer forming at the top of the pack and improves body and taste. It sits before or after the final pasteurisation section depending on the design, and it is one of the most energy-intensive machines on the line, so its capacity is chosen precisely for the real throughput rather than with an inflated margin.

5. Pasteurisation or heat treatment

Milk is heated in a plate heat exchanger to a set temperature and time to kill pathogens, then cooled immediately. The exchanger has a regeneration section that recovers a large share of the energy by exchanging heat between the hot outgoing and cold incoming streams — the single biggest factor in the line's energy use.

6. Manufacture and filling

The route branches by product, and each branch imposes its own equipment, time and space:

  • Liquid milk goes from pasteurisation and cooling straight to a balance tank and then to the filling machine.
  • Yoghurt is cooled to incubation temperature, inoculated with a starter, and fermented in tanks or in the pack depending on the type (stirred or set), then cooled quickly to stop acidity at the target point.
  • Cheese goes through coagulation with rennet and starter, cutting the curd, draining the whey, pressing, salting, and sometimes ripening in controlled rooms.
  • Butter is churned from pasteurised cream until the fat grains separate, then washed, worked and packed.

After manufacture, products are filled in the chosen pack, then moved by conveyor to secondary packing and cold storage. The packaging method is fixed early because it dictates the type of pasteurisation, the shelf life and the shape of the distribution chain.

Core equipment on the line

The following list is the minimum for a liquid-milk line; each additional product adds its own equipment. The most important thing when assessing any proposal is that these machines are capacity-matched to each other: a separator or homogeniser smaller than the pasteuriser creates a bottleneck, and an exchanger larger than the filling runs at partial load.

  • Chilled intake and storage tanks with insulation, agitators and level and temperature sensors, sized to cover the largest expected daily delivery.
  • Strainers and a centrifugal clarifier to remove sediment before processing.
  • Separator to split cream and standardise fat content.
  • Homogeniser, high-pressure, to make fat globules uniform.
  • Plate heat exchanger for pasteurisation, with a regeneration section.
  • Process and fermentation tanks, temperature-controlled, for products such as yoghurt.
  • CIP unit with alkaline and acid solution tanks, heating and pumps.
  • Filling machines matched to the pack format: pouches, plastic bottles, cartons, cups.
  • Conveyors linking filling to packing and storage; see conveyor systems for choosing a type suited to a washdown environment.

Cleaning-in-place (CIP) and hygienic design

Milk is an ideal growth medium, and residue in any dead leg inside a pipe or valve spoils the next production run. CIP is therefore not an add-on; the line is designed around it from the start: pipework with correct drainage slopes, hygienic fittings with no crevices, valves that can be cleaned automatically, and smooth internal surfaces. The CIP unit circulates alkaline then acid wash then a hot-water rinse in programmed cycles, and their temperature, time and concentration are logged as part of the food-safety system.

Poor hygienic design shows up late: recurring contamination the testing does not explain, and cleaning time that eats into production time. Correcting it after installation is expensive, so it is worth scrutinising in the technical proposal before purchase.

Utilities and infrastructure the line needs

Equipment alone does not run a line. A dairy line needs:

  • Steam from a boiler for pasteurisation, sterilisation and heating CIP solutions.
  • Chilled water from a refrigeration unit to cool milk after treatment and hold tanks.
  • Compressed air, dry and clean, to operate valves and filling equipment.
  • Potable water in quantity for rinsing and CIP, with treatment if required.
  • Effluent treatment able to handle a high organic load before discharge.
  • Electrical supply sized for pumps, refrigeration and compressors, with a backup source for product under treatment.

Underestimating the size of these utilities is a common reason for delayed start-up after the equipment arrives. Ask the supplier for a consumption schedule for each utility at maximum capacity.

How to size the line and product range

This decision comes before equipment selection and governs it. Work through it in order:

  1. First-year products: liquid milk only, or with yoghurt and cheese? Each product adds equipment, space and a cleaning cycle.
  2. Daily volume per product: from confirmed demand, not ambition, with a margin for growth and losses.
  3. Operating pattern: one shift or two? It affects the instantaneous capacity needed for pasteurisation and filling.
  4. Heat treatment: pasteurisation with a cold chain, or UHT with aseptic filling for a long ambient shelf life.
  5. Expansion path: ask the supplier what is replaced and what is added when capacity is doubled later.

A line larger than needed raises energy, water and cleaning time with no return, and lengthens the payback period. Our engineers can review these factors against your figures on a site visit — request a quotation.

Regulatory requirements in Egypt

Dairy-manufacturing facilities are overseen by the National Food Safety Authority, alongside industrial and environmental licensing bodies. General considerations include approved food-contact materials, separation of clean and unclean routes, a documented cold chain, heat-treatment and cleaning records, and effluent treatment. These requirements and their detail can be updated; verify them with the authority directly before contracting for equipment, and do not base the decision on an old specification or a supplier's word. Make the technical proposal's compliance with these requirements a written clause in the contract.

What drives the cost of a dairy production line

There is no single price; cost is built from elements you can estimate in advance:

  • Daily capacity: sets the size of pasteurisation, tanks, pumps and filling.
  • Product range: each additional product means its own equipment, space and cleaning cycle.
  • Heat-treatment type: UHT and aseptic filling cost more than ordinary pasteurisation.
  • Automation level: full control and data logging versus semi-manual operation.
  • Utilities: boiler, refrigeration unit, compressor and effluent treatment are separate line items often overlooked.
  • Supply and spares: local availability of spare parts and support lowers lifetime downtime cost.

The useful comparison is not between two line prices, but between the full cost over the line's life: equipment plus utilities plus energy and water plus labour plus losses plus maintenance.

Common mistakes when setting up a dairy line

  • Sizing equipment to ambition, not demand, so the line runs at a costly partial load.
  • Overlooking utility sizing, so the equipment arrives but cannot run until the boiler and refrigeration are complete.
  • Treating CIP as a later add-on instead of designing the line to be cleanable in place.
  • Filling speed not matched to conveyor speed, so product accumulates or starves the feed; details on the technical support page.
  • Not tying regulatory compliance to the contract, so gaps appear after installation that are hard to correct.

These are items that can be avoided at the technical-proposal stage. If you are planning a new line or an expansion, our team can review the proposed production line in full before you contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the stages of a dairy production line? +
In sequence: chilled raw-milk intake and quality testing, filtration and clarification, cold storage, separation and fat standardisation, homogenisation, pasteurisation or heat treatment, product manufacture (liquid milk, yoghurt, cheese, butter or cream), then filling, packing, cooling and storage. A cleaning-in-place system and utilities — steam, chilled water, compressed air — run across all stages.
What is the difference between pasteurisation and UHT treatment on dairy lines? +
Pasteurisation heats milk to a lower temperature for a comparatively longer hold, killing pathogens but leaving a short shelf life that needs a cold chain. UHT raises the temperature for a few seconds then cools immediately, with aseptic filling, giving a long ambient shelf life in exchange for more costly and complex equipment and packaging.
What is CIP and why is it essential in a dairy plant? +
CIP, or cleaning-in-place, circulates alkaline and acid wash solutions and hot water through tanks, pipework and heat exchangers without dismantling them. Milk is an ideal growth medium, and residue in any dead leg spoils the next batch, so the line is designed from the start to be fully cleanable in place, with CIP cycles logged as part of the food-safety system.
What core equipment does a small dairy line need? +
A chilled intake tank and strainer, a plate heat exchanger for pasteurisation with a heat-recovery section, a homogeniser, a separator for fat standardisation, jacketed process and storage tanks, a CIP unit, and a filling machine suited to the product. Yoghurt adds temperature-controlled fermentation tanks; cheese adds a vat and draining and pressing equipment.
How do I choose the right dairy line capacity? +
Start from confirmed demand, not from the maximum the budget allows. Fix the first-year products and the daily volume of each, add a margin for growth and losses, then ask the supplier to size pasteurisation, tanks and filling to that figure and to state the effect of expanding later. A line larger than needed raises running and cleaning cost with no return.
Which authority licenses dairy factories in Egypt? +
The National Food Safety Authority oversees food-manufacturing facilities including dairy, alongside industrial and environmental licensing bodies. Requirements for food-contact materials and the cold chain can be updated, so verify them with the authority directly before buying equipment rather than relying on an old specification or a supplier's word.
