Oil Filling Line: Stages, Equipment and Cost Drivers

A practical guide to the oil filling line: line stages, choosing a filling method by viscosity, equipment, capping and sealing, and what drives cost.

← Back to all articles

PRODUCTION LINES ·29 August 2026 ·9 min read

An oil filling line is a connected series of equipment that moves an empty container, fills it with a controlled quantity of oil, then caps it, seals it, applies a label and production date, and delivers it ready for distribution. It typically runs through six stages: container feeding and orientation, volumetric or net-weight filling, cap placement, cap tightening, labelling and coding, then secondary packing. The details of the line change substantially with the oil type — edible or lubricant — its viscosity, the container size, and the required output.

This guide is written for anyone specifying a new filling line or upgrading an existing one. It focuses on the engineering decisions that determine whether the line works before it is installed, rather than on machine datasheets.

The stages, in order

The sequence is almost fixed across lines. What changes is the size of each unit, how it works, and the degree of automation:

  1. Container feeding and orientation from a rotary table or an unscrambler that arranges containers and delivers them to the conveyor in a single lane.
  2. Filling to a controlled quantity per container, volumetric, flow-meter or net-weight depending on the oil.
  3. Cap placement with automatic cap feeding from a sorting bowl.
  4. Cap tightening with a capping head at a set torque, then an induction foil seal where required.
  5. Labelling and coding with the production date and batch number on the container or cap.
  6. Secondary packing into cases or onto pallets ahead of storage and shipping.

A filtration or de-aeration step may be added before filling, and edible-oil lines sometimes add container drying or sterilisation.

Edible oil or lubricant: the decision that shapes the line

The first decision that determines the whole line is the target oil type, because it sets the product-contact material and the hygiene requirements:

  • Edible oils require food-grade stainless product-contact parts and food-contact-approved seals, a clean-in-place (CIP) system to prevent rancidity and oxidised residue build-up, and a clear separation between the empty-container and filled-container paths.
  • Lubricants and automotive oils tolerate less demanding materials, but are often higher viscosity, contain additives that can settle, and may involve handling semi-solid greases in some products.

Converting a line built for one type to the other is not a small change: a lubricant line cannot fill edible oil without replacing the product-contact parts entirely, and the reverse is expensive for no reason. Settle this before the line is designed, not after.

Filling method: the most important decision on an oil line

Oil viscosity governs the choice of filling method, and there are four main options:

  • Piston (volumetric) filling is accurate with medium and high viscosity oils, handles thick products and greases, but is relatively slow and needs longer cleaning at product changeover.
  • Flow-meter filling suits low to medium viscosity edible oils, is fast and easy to adjust digitally, but its accuracy is affected by viscosity changing with temperature.
  • Net-weight filling delivers a controlled weight directly, regardless of viscosity and temperature, and is preferred for large containers and drums and where products are sold by the kilogram.
  • Gravity or pressure filling is simpler and cheaper, and only suits very light oils and containers with a wide tolerance.

Choosing a method that does not match the real viscosity range of the planned products is the most common oil-line design mistake, and it shows up as inconsistent fill levels after start-up, not before.

Viscosity and temperature: why some oils need heating

Oil viscosity changes sharply with temperature, which has practical consequences for line design:

  • High-viscosity oils such as gear oils and some engine oils need tanks and pipework with heating jackets to hold a stable filling viscosity.
  • Seasonal plant-temperature swings between summer and winter change filling speed and accuracy on flow-meter lines; the fix is temperature conditioning of the product before filling, or a method that is not affected by viscosity.
  • Some edible oils can cloud or deposit sediment when cold, calling for gentle warming and suitable holding before filling to keep the appearance right.

Ignoring this leaves a well-designed line producing results that vary with the weather alone.

Fill nozzles, drip control and foaming

Oil strings and drips after filling unless the nozzles are set up for it, contaminating the cap, the label and the work area:

  • Dive nozzles descend into the container and rise with the oil level to reduce foaming and splashing — close to mandatory with foamy oils or fast filling.
  • Anti-drip nozzles with a fast shut-off valve or suck-back at the end of each dose.
  • A no-bottle-no-fill sensor stops the nozzle opening when no container is present, saving product and keeping the line clean.

These are low-cost details, but skipping them turns the line into a source of daily loss and repeated cleaning stops.

Container type shapes the line

The range and type of containers sets the number of filling heads and the conveyor design:

  • Small retail bottles allow linear or rotary multi-head lines at high speed.
  • Jerricans in mid-range sizes need fewer, larger filling heads, and often two stages: a fast fill followed by a slow top-up to set the level accurately.
  • Drums and large containers need net-weight filling on a floor scale with a manual or semi-automatic filling lance.

A line designed for one size is hard to adapt later to sizes far from it; define the full container range before purchase.

Capping and sealing systems

The cap type determines the closing unit required:

  • Plastic screw caps are applied with a capping head at a set torque; too much torque cracks the cap, too little leaks.
  • ROPP metal caps are formed onto the container neck during closing and need dedicated forming heads.
  • Induction foil sealing is placed under the cap and heat-welded for tamper evidence and to prevent leakage in transit — common on both edible and automotive oil.

Combining a capping head and an induction sealer on the same line is a decision to take at the start, because adding it later means rearranging the units along the conveyor.

Equipment on an oil filling line

  • Rotary feed table or unscrambler for empty containers.
  • Product tank in stainless steel, with a heating jacket if needed, and a transfer pump matched to the viscosity.
  • Filtration and de-aeration unit where required.
  • Filling machine, volumetric, flow-meter or net-weight depending on the product.
  • Cap feeding unit and capping head at a set torque.
  • Induction sealing unit where foil seals are used.
  • Labelling machine, wrap-around or front-and-back.
  • Coding printer for production date and batch number.
  • Conveyors, dividers and accumulators between units.
  • Secondary packing unit and case assembly.

Conveyor systems link these units together, and mismatched speeds between them are a common cause of stoppages on lines whose individual machines are all working correctly.

Filtration and de-aeration before filling

On edible-oil lines specifically, the clarity and appearance of the oil affect consumer acceptance:

  • Final filtration removes any fine impurities or residual particles before the oil enters the filling tank.
  • De-aeration reduces dissolved oxygen, which accelerates oxidation and rancidity, and cuts foaming during fast filling.

These two units are sometimes left out to cut cost, and the need for them then appears after the first complaint about cloudiness or short shelf life.

Degree of automation: semi-automatic, linear, or rotary monobloc

  • Semi-automatic: one or two filling heads with manual capping, suited to limited output and a high variety of products.
  • Linear automatic line: separate units on a straight conveyor, easier to maintain and extend later.
  • Rotary monobloc: filling and capping in one frame, higher speed and a smaller footprint, but less flexible and higher spare-parts cost.

The choice follows from the target output and how stable it is, not from wanting the highest possible speed.

Coding and labelling

Labelling and coding are the last two stages before packing, and the most prone to visual defects:

  • Wrap-around labels suit cylindrical containers, and front-and-back labels suit flat ones.
  • Coding with the production and expiry dates and batch number needs to be clear, permanent and resistant to oil contact.
  • An inspection camera or sensor confirming label presence and code accuracy reduces returns.

Oil on the container before the label is applied stops it adhering, which is why many lines wipe or dry the container after filling.

What actually drives the cost

There is no standard price for an oil filling line. Any figure quoted without the following is guesswork:

  • Output in containers per hour — the single largest factor.
  • Viscosity range of the target products, which sets the filling method and the type of pumps and pipework.
  • Range of container sizes and their number; each distant size adds changeover tooling.
  • Grade of product-contact parts — food or industrial — and whether the line needs a CIP system.
  • Need for heating jackets on the tank and pipework.
  • Degree of automation and the type of capping and sealing unit.
  • Equipment origin and local spare-parts availability and lead time.

That last point is consistently underweighted: cheaper equipment with a long import lead time on spares can end up costing more at the first breakdown, because a stopped line is measured in lost production, not the price of the part.

Size changeover and product changeover time

Oil lines often run more than one product and more than one size, and the changeover time between them is a real operating cost:

  • Size changeover means adjusting nozzle heights, conveyor guides, capping heads and the fill program.
  • Product changeover between oils of different colour or viscosity means draining and cleaning the line to prevent mixing.
  • Tool-less quick-change parts and pre-calibrated positions cut downtime noticeably.

Asking the supplier, in writing, for the expected changeover time in each case reveals large differences between lines that look identical on paper.

Regulatory requirements in Egypt

The regulator depends on the oil type:

  • Edible oils are a regulated food activity under the National Food Safety Authority, alongside labelling and product standards from the Egyptian Organization for Standardization and Quality.
  • Lubricants and automotive oils fall under petroleum-sector and standards-and-quality requirements, plus the usual industrial and environmental licensing.
  • The declared fill quantity on the container is subject to standards oversight and legal metrology.

Requirements are updated periodically, so check with the relevant authorities directly before execution. Settling the product classification and labelling requirements in writing with the project team before signing any purchase contract avoids costly changes after installation.

Utilities and losses: the hidden line items

Offers are normally compared on purchase price alone, while the real cost of running an oil filling line shows up monthly:

  • Compressed air to drive valves and cylinders, with load rising as the number of heads increases.
  • Electrical load under full running conditions, not the nameplate rating.
  • Heating energy for the tank and pipework on high-viscosity lines.
  • Oil lost to drips, line draining at product changeover, and pipework residue.

Two lines with the same speed can differ considerably on these items, and the difference accumulates over the working life of the line.

Common mistakes when buying an oil filling line

  • Specifying equipment before settling the oil type, its viscosity range and its container sizes.
  • Choosing flow-meter filling for products whose viscosity changes a lot with temperature.
  • Leaving out heating jackets on a line that will fill high-viscosity oils.
  • Failing to match the filling unit speed to the capping and labelling units.
  • Underestimating container drying before labelling, so adhesion problems recur.
  • Choosing a supplier with no fast local technical support.

The last carries the heaviest consequences; a line that cannot get an engineer the same day turns from a productive asset into a liability, which is why technical support and spare-parts availability belong in the purchase decision rather than being treated as an afterthought.

Maintenance after start-up

Maintenance on oil lines is preventive by nature, because leaks and drips get worse over time. A reasonable minimum includes:

  • Inspecting piston, valve and nozzle-port seals regularly and replacing them before they leak.
  • Checking fill quantity against a reference scale at the start of every run.
  • Monitoring capping-head torque to keep a consistent seal.
  • Holding stock of fast-wearing spare parts: seals, nozzles, belts and sealing heads.

Before you request a quotation

Prepare four things: the target oil or oils and their viscosity range, the range of container sizes, the required output in containers per hour, and the cap and seal type. With those figures, a quotation becomes realistic and genuinely comparable between suppliers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the stages of an oil filling line? +
Six, in sequence: unscrambling and orienting empty containers into a single lane, filling each container to a controlled quantity, cap placement, cap tightening to a set torque with an induction foil seal where required, labelling and date/batch coding, then secondary packing into cases or onto pallets. A filtration or de-aeration step is often added before filling on edible-oil lines, along with container drying.
Which filling method suits oil best? +
Viscosity decides. Piston (volumetric) filling handles medium to high viscosity oils and greases accurately but is slower and needs longer cleaning at product changeover. Flow-meter filling suits low to medium viscosity edible oils, is fast and easy to set digitally, but its accuracy drifts as temperature changes viscosity. Net-weight filling delivers a fixed weight regardless of viscosity and is preferred for large containers and drums.
Can one line fill both edible oil and motor oil? +
Not without a substantial rebuild. An edible-oil line uses food-grade stainless product-contact parts, food-contact-approved seals and a clean-in-place system, while a lubricant line is built with industrial-grade materials for higher viscosities. Switching between the two means replacing the product-contact parts entirely, which is why the oil type is settled before the line is designed.
What determines the cost of an oil filling line? +
Seven main factors: output in containers per hour, the viscosity range of the products, the range and number of container sizes, the grade of product-contact materials and whether the line needs a CIP system, whether the tank and pipework need heating jackets, the degree of automation and the type of capping and sealing unit, and the equipment origin with local spare-parts availability. A line cannot be priced without these.
