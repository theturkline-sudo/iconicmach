Juice Production Line: Stages, Equipment and Cost Drivers

A practical guide to the juice production line — NFC vs. concentrate, pasteurisation methods, equipment, packaging choice, and what actually drives cost.

← Back to all articles

PRODUCTION LINES ·24 August 2026 ·8 min read

A juice production line is a connected series of equipment that turns fresh fruit or fruit concentrate into a sealed, packed juice ready for sale. It typically runs through eight stages: fruit intake, sorting and washing, peeling and cutting, extraction, filtration, pasteurisation, filling, then storage. The details of the line depend on the fruit used, whether the target product is NFC or reconstituted from concentrate, and the pasteurisation method chosen.

This guide is written for anyone specifying a new line or upgrading an existing one. It focuses on the engineering decisions that determine whether the project succeeds, rather than on machine datasheets.

The stages, in order

  1. Fruit intake and inspection from approved suppliers, checking for quality and damage.
  2. Sorting and washing to remove dirt, debris and unsuitable fruit.
  3. Peeling and cutting, depending on the fruit — skipped entirely for some varieties.
  4. Extraction or pressing to separate juice from pulp, seeds and skins.
  5. Filtration to set the pulp content of the final product.
  6. Pasteurisation by heat, to control microbial load and extend shelf life.
  7. Filling and packing into the final container, sterile or hot-filled.
  8. Storage, chilled or at room temperature depending on the pasteurisation and filling method.

NFC or from concentrate: the decision that shapes the whole line

The first decision that determines the entire line design is the target product type. NFC (Not From Concentrate) juice is extracted from fresh fruit and packed without evaporating any water out of it, which means extraction and pasteurisation need to sit close in time to the fruit source, and the cold chain has to be strong enough to hold quality through to filling.

Juice from concentrate adds an evaporation stage after extraction that reduces the liquid volume, cutting transport and storage cost, before water is added back immediately before filling. That path needs an evaporator that an NFC line does not, but it buys flexibility to produce outside the fruit's harvest season.

Mixing the two approaches after equipment has already been bought is an expensive fix. This has to be settled at the planning stage, not after installation.

Pasteurisation method and what it means for filling

The pasteurisation method chosen decides the filling machine and the shelf life:

  • HTST (High Temperature Short Time) suits chilled, shorter shelf-life products distributed through a cold chain.
  • UHT with aseptic filling gives a long room-temperature shelf life, but the aseptic filler carries a higher price tag.
  • Hot-fill, where juice is filled hot into its final container and the bottle self-sterilises as it cools — common on PET lines.

Choosing a method that does not match the intended distribution channel is a recurring mistake that only becomes visible after start-up.

Equipment on a juice production line

  • Sorting and washing machine for raw fruit.
  • Peeling and cutting unit, where needed.
  • Extraction or pressing unit matched to the fruit type.
  • Filtration unit to set pulp content.
  • Heat exchanger for pasteurisation, or a UHT unit depending on the chosen path.
  • Stainless sterilisation and blending tanks.
  • Filling machine, hot-fill or aseptic depending on pasteurisation method.
  • Capping and coding machine for date and batch marking.
  • Final packing unit.

Conveyor systems link these units together, and mismatched speeds between them are a common cause of stoppages on lines whose individual machines are all working correctly.

What actually drives the cost

There is no standard price for a juice line. Any figure quoted without the following is guesswork:

  • Throughput in litres per hour — the single largest factor.
  • Target fruit and the resulting juice viscosity — mango juice needs different pumps and piping than clear apple juice.
  • Pasteurisation and filling method — aseptic filling costs more than hot-fill.
  • Degree of automation — semi-automatic against fully automatic.
  • Final packaging format and the number of pack sizes.
  • Equipment origin and whether spare parts are available locally.

That last point is consistently underweighted. Cheaper equipment with a long import lead time on spares can end up costing more at the first breakdown, because a stopped line is measured in lost production, not the price of the part.

Choosing the right throughput

The instinct is to buy the largest capacity affordable, and on a juice line specifically that is often the wrong call because of fruit seasonality. The right figure comes from three numbers: realistic monthly market demand, the hours the plant will actually run across the full year rather than just at peak season, and expected availability.

A high-capacity line running at full output for only a few months a year is not an efficient investment. It is usually better to size for regular running and leave physical space in the layout for an additional unit as demand grows.

Choosing a packaging format

Packaging is a marketing decision and an engineering one at the same time, and it is set mainly by the distribution channel:

  • PET bottles suit hot-fill and short-chain retail.
  • Aseptic cartons suit unrefrigerated distribution over longer distances and longer shelf life.
  • Pouches suit bulk or secondary packaging for the food service sector.

Each format needs a different filling machine, so changing the decision after the line is bought means replacing a whole unit, not making a small adjustment.

The seasonality problem

This is routinely left out of planning, despite being the biggest factor in how often the line actually runs across the year. Three practical ways to manage it:

  • Contracting suppliers across different growing regions to extend the fresh-fruit window.
  • Using frozen pulp or concentrate as a raw material outside harvest season.
  • Sizing cold storage into the plant layout from the initial design, not as an afterthought.

A line sized for peak capacity with no plan for running outside the harvest window turns into idle capital for much of the year.

Space and layout

Layout precedes machine selection. It needs to separate clearly: fruit intake and cold storage close to the plant entrance; the processing and extraction hall with epoxy flooring and drainage sized for high water volumes; the filling hall, ideally isolated to limit contamination; and finished goods storage, chilled or ambient depending on the pasteurisation method.

Space also has to be reserved for a CIP (clean-in-place) room, because juice lines need more frequent cleaning than most food lines — fruit fibre and pulp residue builds up in pipework and tanks.

Regulatory requirements in Egypt

Juice manufacturing is a regulated food activity under the National Food Safety Authority, alongside standards from the Egyptian Organization for Standardization and Quality that classify the product by actual fruit content, plus the usual industrial licensing.

Check with the relevant authorities directly before execution, since requirements are updated periodically, and the target product category affects the line design itself — the required pasteurisation intensity and packaging type — which is hard to change after installation.

Juice, nectar, or fruit drink

The distinction between these categories is regulatory, not marketing, and it is set primarily by actual fruit content and whether water or sugar has been added. The target category has to be settled before the line is designed, because each one can require a different dilution or sweetening step at the blending stage before filling, and each is subject to different manufacturing and labelling requirements set by the relevant regulatory bodies.

Common mistakes when buying a juice line

  • Choosing equipment before settling the target fruit and the NFC-or-concentrate path.
  • Ignoring fruit seasonality when sizing throughput.
  • Underestimating CIP cleaning requirements in the space plan.
  • Failing to match extraction speed to filling speed, leaving a row of separate machines.
  • Choosing a supplier with no local technical support.

The last carries the heaviest consequences. A line that cannot get an engineer the same day turns from a productive asset into a liability, which is why technical support and spare parts availability belong in the purchase decision rather than being treated as an afterthought.

Maintenance after start-up

Maintenance on a juice line is preventive by nature, because any stoppage leaves product inside the line that may spoil before running resumes. A reasonable minimum includes a documented CIP cleaning schedule after every batch, tracking filter and screen wear, logging motor current to catch degradation early, and holding stock of fast-wearing spare parts such as gaskets and screens.

Utilities: the cost that shows up monthly

Offers are normally compared on purchase price, while the real cost appears in the monthly running bill. Ask every supplier, in writing, for:

  • Steam or thermal energy demand for the pasteurisation stage, which matters most on UHT lines.
  • Electrical load per unit under full running conditions, not nameplate rating.
  • CIP water consumption per cycle, given how much more often a juice line needs cleaning than most food lines.
  • Refrigeration load required for finished-goods storage where a chilled path is used.

Two lines with identical throughput can differ considerably on these figures, and the difference repeats for the working life of the plant.

Before you request a quotation

Prepare three things: the target fruit or fruits, the required throughput in litres per hour, and the intended final packaging format. With those figures, a quotation becomes realistic and genuinely comparable between suppliers.
